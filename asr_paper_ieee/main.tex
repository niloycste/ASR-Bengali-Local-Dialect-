\begin{abstract}
We introduce AncholVox, a silver-standard diagnostic benchmark for
multi-dialect Bengali ASR and Bengali--English code-switching detected in
automatic transcripts. It contains $41{,}499$ clips (131.2 hours) from 15
regional labels, constructed through speech segmentation, Whisper large-v3
transcription, script-based switch detection, and text-only LLM filtering. On
the archived historical split, fine-tuned wav2vec2 achieves the lowest primary
WER against the silver references ($0.861$). These model comparisons are
diagnostic because that split is not source-disjoint. In a limited 100-clip,
two-annotator validation study, MMS-1B has the lowest WER against both human
references ($0.757$ and $0.697$), showing that rankings depend on the reference.
AncholVox is therefore presented as an auditable dataset and stress-test
benchmark for silver-label noise, dialect variation, decoder repetition, and
English-token retention, not as a new state-of-the-art ASR model.\footnote{Dataset: \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/niloycste68/Bangali_local_dialect_ASR_HF_Dataset} --- gated access pending the institutional determinations recorded in the release register; code: \url{https://github.com/niloycste/ASR-Bengali-Local-Dialect-}.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Automatic speech recognition (ASR) does not work equally well across languages,
regional varieties, and speaking styles. Dialect variation and code-switching
(CS), in which speakers alternate between languages within an utterance, are
particularly challenging when annotated speech is scarce.

Bengali has substantial regional variation, including Chittagonian, Sylheti,
Noakhali, Rangpuri, and Barishali varieties, which differ in pronunciation,
vocabulary, and expression. Informal online speech may also contain Bengali and
English words in the same utterance. For example, a speaker may say,
`\textit{chatgaiya bhashay phone unboxing kortachi, honestly speaking camera
performance onek bhalo},'' meaning `unboxing a phone in the Chittagong dialect;
honestly speaking, the camera performance is very good.''

Despite this, most existing Bengali speech datasets focus on only one problem at a time. For example, read-speech datasets like OpenSLR-37~\cite{kjartansson2018bengali} mainly cover standard Bengali. The Ben-10 corpus~\cite{ben10_2025} includes ten regional dialects but does not include code-switching. On the other hand, the MUCS shared task~\cite{mucs2021} provides Bengali--English code-switching data, but only in standard Dhaka Bengali. We are not aware of another released resource at this scale that jointly targets broad regional variation and Bengali--English switching detected in silver transcripts. This gap between real-world speech and available resources makes it difficult to study robust ASR systems.


Recognizing dialectal code-switched speech is difficult for several reasons. First, regional dialects can be quite different from standard Bengali in pronunciation, sound patterns, rhythm, and vocabulary, so models trained on standard Bengali do not perform well. Second, code-switching requires the system to handle smooth switching between Bengali and English within the same sentence, often mixing languages that standard monolingual models are not designed for. Third, there is very little training data for dialectal and code-switched speech, and manually transcribing data across 15 dialects is very expensive. Finally, many dialect words and borrowed English terms do not have fixed spellings, so even correct transcriptions may be marked wrong by strict evaluation metrics. These challenges together motivate the design of our dataset, pipeline, and evaluation approach. They lead us to build an automated annotation pipeline without manual transcription, use script-aware code-switch detection, adopt a shared vocabulary sequence-to-sequence model, and focus on character-level and code-switch-aware evaluation metrics.


This work fills this gap; Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} provides an end-to-end overview of the dataset construction, models, and evaluation. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item One of the first large-scale resources designed specifically for the intersection of regional Bengali dialects and Bengali--English switching detected in silver transcripts: 41{,}499 clips (131.2 hours) across 15 regional labels.

\item A scalable automated construction pipeline using voice-activity segmentation, Whisper-based silver transcription, script-based code-switch detection, and text-only multilingual LLM review of borderline cases, complemented by a two-annotator validation subset.

\item A diagnostic benchmark comparing three ASR model families under zero-shot and fine-tuned settings, including Bengali-only, code-switch-only, and joint-data training recipes. Because these recipes differ in coverage and data budget, they provide practical comparisons rather than a controlled causal estimate of each data type.

\item A detailed evaluation and error analysis across dialects and code-switching conditions, including two-sided significance tests, bootstrap uncertainty for code-switching metrics, and an LLM-ensemble meaning-preservation diagnostic.
\end{enumerate}

AncholVox is positioned primarily as a dataset-construction and auditability
contribution. The model experiments are diagnostic stress tests of the released
resource, not an exhaustive state-of-the-art leaderboard. Their purpose is to
measure failure modes under a common protocol and to separate model behavior
from silver-reference noise, which is characterized on a limited sample in
Section~\ref{sec:validation}.


The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on Bengali, dialectal, and code-switched ASR. Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the AncholVox dataset and its construction process. Section~\ref{sec:models} presents the ASR models and fine-tuning strategy, while Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the main results, Section~\ref{sec:analysis} provides detailed error analysis, training-recipe comparisons, and case studies, Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses limitations and reproducibility, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper with future directions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews prior studies on Bengali automatic speech recognition (ASR), covering standard and dialectal ASR, code-switched speech recognition, large scale pretrained speech models, automated speech corpus construction, and large language model (LLM)-based data quality assessment. While recent advances in multilingual and low-resource ASR have improved speech recognition performance across many languages, existing Bengali ASR research has largely focused on standard Bengali speech. As a result, the combined challenges of regional dialect variation and Bengali--English code-switching remain underexplored.


\subsection{Bengali and Dialectal ASR}
Early Bengali ASR systems were mainly based on Gaussian mixture models and deep neural network hidden Markov models with manually designed pronunciation lexicons~\cite{hasnat2007isolated,swarna2017survey}. These systems mostly focused on standard Bengali and had limited ability to handle unseen words. Later, Bengali ASR research moved toward end to end approaches, including connectionist temporal classification (CTC)~\cite{graves2006ctc}, deep recurrent networks~\cite{graves2013speech}, Deep Speech~\cite{hannun2014deep}, and attention based encoder decoder models such as Listen, Attend and Spell~\cite{chan2016listen}. Toolkits such as ESPnet~\cite{watanabe2018espnet} further supported this progress and helped reduce error rates for standard Bengali ASR~\cite{mandal2020e2e}. More recently, the Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} and masked pre training~\cite{devlin2019bert} have enabled powerful self supervised and weakly supervised speech models, including wav2vec,2.0~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec2}, HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}, WavLM~\cite{chen2022wavlm}, XLS-R~\cite{babu2022xlsr,conneau2021xlsr}, Whisper~\cite{radford2023whisper}, and MMS~\cite{pratap2024mms}. Together with multilingual datasets such as Common Voice~\cite{ardila2020commonvoice}, these models have improved transfer learning for resource limited Bengali speech recognition. However, dialectal Bengali has received focused attention only recently. The Ben-10 corpus~\cite{ben10_2025}, which contains more than 78 hours of speech from 394 speakers across ten regional dialects, shows that even strong foundation models such as Whisper large v3 perform poorly on dialectal speech that differs from the training data, both before and after fine tuning. Biswas \emph{et al.}~\cite{biswas2025unified} propose a WavLM based denoising and adaptation framework for dialectal Bengali, including Chittagonian and Sylheti, under simulated noise, and report large relative reductions in WER compared with wav2vec,2.0 and Whisper at low signal to noise ratios. Ridoy \emph{et al.}~\cite{ridoy2025adaptability} compare Whisper and Wav2Vec-BERT on standard Bangla and find that the self supervised encoder is both more accurate and more computationally efficient in resource limited settings. However, these studies do not address code switching, which motivates the need for a benchmark that brings dialect variation and code switching together in a single resource.


\subsection{Code-Switching ASR}
Code switching, where speakers alternate between two languages within a conversation or even within a single sentence, is common in multilingual communities. It is especially frequent in informal Bangladeshi speech, where English words are often inserted into Bengali sentences. This creates several challenges for ASR systems. First, the model must recognize words from two languages and, in the case of Bengali and English, two different writing systems. Second, code switched speech is still rare in standard ASR training datasets. Third, language switching often occurs together with accent, pronunciation, and acoustic variation, which makes both speech recognition and language modeling more difficult~\cite{yilmaz2018augmentation,sitaram2019survey}. Progress in code switched ASR has largely been supported by dedicated datasets, such as the Mandarin English SEAME corpus~\cite{lyu2010seame}. However, recent studies show that even large multilingual models do not handle code switching reliably unless they are trained with explicit code switched data~\cite{winata2021multilingual}. For Indian languages, the MUCS 2021 challenge~\cite{mucs2021} introduced Bengali English and Hindi English code switching benchmarks, but the Bengali data is limited to standard Dhaka Bengali and does not include regional dialects. One useful feature of Bengali English code switching is that the two languages use different Unicode ranges. This allows word level language identification to be performed almost directly from the script, without requiring a separate language identification model. From a modeling perspective, sequence to sequence models such as Whisper are better suited to this setting because their shared subword vocabulary~\cite{sennrich2016bpe} can represent both scripts and generate mixed script text in a single decoding process. In contrast, monolingual CTC models such as MMS are usually restricted to one output script and therefore cannot properly represent code switched speech. This work evaluates both modeling paradigms to measure this gap in dialectal Bengali ASR.


\subsection{Automated Corpus Construction for Low-Resource ASR}
Since manual transcription is difficult to scale for many low-resource language varieties, recent speech corpora increasingly rely on automated data construction pipelines. For example, GigaSpeech 2~\cite{yang2025gigaspeech2} builds a large multi-domain corpus for Thai, Indonesian, and Vietnamese using automated web crawling, Whisper-based transcription, and iterative refinement. This shows that weakly supervised labels can support large-scale corpus creation without relying entirely on human transcribers. Other studies have also used synthetic speech and spectrogram augmentation to increase the amount of training data for low-resource ASR~\cite{dhahbi2025endtoend}. AncholVox follows this automated approach, but focuses on a previously underexplored setting: multi-dialect Bengali speech with English code switching. It also adds an LLM-based judge to filter borderline or uncertain transcripts.

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge for Data Quality}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as automatic evaluators in natural language processing, including pairwise response judging~\cite{zheng2023judging,wang2023pandalm} and multi-dimensional quality scoring~\cite{fu2023gptscore}. These models are especially useful when human annotation is expensive and reference labels are limited or noisy, as in low-resource dialectal ASR. However, their reliability depends on careful prompt design and the capability of the judging model. In this work, we use LLM judges at two stages. During dataset construction, a strong multilingual model, Qwen2.5~\cite{qwen2025}, evaluates borderline silver-standard transcripts across several quality dimensions and decides whether they should be retained. During evaluation, an ensemble of three open models, Mistral~\cite{jiang2023mistral}, LLaMA~\cite{dubey2024llama}, and Gemma~\cite{gemmateam2025gemma3technicalreport}, provides an additional reference-free assessment that complements word-level metrics, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. To the best of our knowledge, using LLM judges for both quality control and evaluation in code-switched low-resource ASR has not been previously explored.

Qwen2.5 was selected because its open weights permit fixed, local inference:
transcripts need not be sent to a commercial API, and the same model and prompt
can be rerun by other researchers. This choice is operational rather than a
claim that Qwen2.5 is superior to commercial judges or manual review. Moreover,
the construction-stage judge receives text only; it assesses transcript
plausibility and cannot verify acoustic alignment. The threshold cascade and
the independent human sample are therefore essential safeguards rather than
treating the LLM decision as ground truth.

Recent multilingual systems provide additional context for the experimental
scope. SeamlessM4T supports Bengali as an input speech language and is a
relevant future cross-architecture baseline~\cite{seamlessm4t2023}. Canary-1B is
a strong multilingual ASR model, but its released language set is limited to
English, German, French, and Spanish and therefore does not provide a Bengali
decoding condition~\cite{puvvada2024canary}. The present suite is consequently
representative rather than exhaustive.


\subsection{Comparison with Existing ASR Resources}
Table~\ref{tab:relwork} summarizes representative Bengali and code-switching ASR resources based on the dimensions most relevant to this study. The comparison highlights two clear gaps in the existing literature. First, standard Bengali ASR corpora and models~\cite{hasnat2007isolated,swarna2017survey,mandal2020e2e,kjartansson2018bengali} mainly focus on standard speech and do not address either regional dialect variation or code switching. Second, existing specialized resources cover only one of these challenges at a time. The Ben-10 corpus~\cite{ben10_2025} includes multiple Bengali dialects but does not contain code-switched speech, while the MUCS task~\cite{mucs2021} provides Bengali English code-switching data only for standard Bengali. Although large multilingual models such as XLS-R, Whisper, and MMS~\cite{babu2022xlsr,radford2023whisper,pratap2024mms} support transfer to Bengali, their performance often drops on dialectal speech. In addition, monolingual CTC models cannot properly generate mixed-script code-switched output. AncholVox addresses this gap by focusing on the intersection of these two underexplored problems: multi-dialect Bengali speech and Bengali--English switching detected in silver transcripts. To the best of our knowledge, it is one of the first resources designed specifically for this setting.

\section{The AncholVox Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:dataset}
AncholVox is designed to study regional Bengali speech and Bengali--English
switching detected in automatically generated transcripts. It combines
dialect-targeted social-media collection with automatic segmentation,
transcription, script-based labeling, and quality filtering. The search design
increases the likelihood of retrieving informal regional speech, but neither a
query label nor a silver transcript independently verifies a speaker's dialect
or the words present in the audio. AncholVox is therefore presented as a large,
auditable diagnostic dataset rather than a manually verified corpus.


\subsection{Dataset Design Principles}
AncholVox is built on three principles. First, dialect-name queries are used to
increase regional coverage because region-name queries, such as ``Chittagong
vlog,'' may retrieve standard Bengali intended for a broad audience. Second,
genres likely to contain informal Bengali--English mixing are prioritized, and
switching labels are subsequently derived from the silver transcripts. Third,
the construction pipeline is automated so that the corpus can be expanded
without requiring manual transcription of every clip. These principles guide
collection; they do not replace acoustic or speaker-level verification.


\subsection{Dialect-Aware Search Query Design}

Search queries determine which creators and speech styles are retrieved.
AncholVox therefore combines dialect-name queries with content genres likely to
contain informal speech and English terms. For each regional label, around
twelve queries are designed across the five categories in
Table~\ref{tab:queries}. Street interviews and live reactions tend to contain
unscripted responses, while technology, gaming, campus, and career content
often contains English terminology. Vlogs add relaxed conversational speech.
Videos uploaded before 2021 and videos with very low viewership are excluded.

        \State $\mathcal{A} \gets \mathcal{A} \cup \{c\}$ \Comment{$+1{,}727$}

        \State do not add $c$ to $\mathcal{A}$ \Comment{REJECT/UNCERTAIN clips conservatively discarded}

\EndFor

\ForAll{$c \in \mathcal{A}$}

\paragraph*{Step 1: Data Acquisition and Segmentation}

Audio is collected from social media platforms, including YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook, using yt-dlp. All audio is downloaded in 16 kHz mono WAV format. A shared de-duplication archive is used to ensure that the same video is not collected more than once. Since social media recordings often contain long silent or non-speech parts, each recording is divided into shorter 5--15 s speech clips using WebRTC voice activity detection. This step produces the audited total of 52{,}020 clips (165.23 hours). Language filtering after transcription retains 48{,}772 clips (155.08 hours), of which 48{,}484 fall below the upper no-speech threshold and enter the confidence cascade described below.

\paragraph*{Step 2: Transcription}

Each clip is transcribed using Whisper large-v3~\cite{radford2023whisper}, with the language setting forced to Bengali. It is used as a reproducible, openly available multilingual labeler; its output remains a noisy substitute for manual transcription in this difficult setting.

\paragraph*{Step 3: Code-Switch Detection}

Code-switching is detected using Unicode script information. Bengali words use the Bengali Unicode range (U+0980--U+09FF), while English words usually appear in Latin script. Since these scripts are separate, word-level language identification can be performed almost directly from the script, without using a separate language identification model.

Let $n_{\mathrm{bn}}$ be the number of Bengali-script words, $n_{\mathrm{en}}$ be the number of Latin-script words, $b$ be the number of language boundaries between adjacent words of different scripts, and $\rho = n_{\mathrm{en}}/(n_{\mathrm{en}}+n_{\mathrm{bn}})$ be the English-word ratio. A clip is labeled as code-switched if it satisfies the following conditions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(n_{\mathrm{bn}} + n_{\mathrm{en}}) \ge 4,\quad
n_{\mathrm{bn}} \ge 2,\quad
n_{\mathrm{en}} \ge 1,\\
b \ge 1,\quad
0.05 \le \rho \le 0.80 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:csrule}
\end{equation}

A stricter high-confidence subset is also created by requiring $n_{\mathrm{en}} \ge 2$ and $0.10 \le \rho \le 0.70$. During automatic annotation, an additional conservative QC check excludes CS candidates with $\rho>0.65$ from automatic acceptance. Thus, 0.80 is the detector's broad labeling boundary, whereas 0.65 is the release-stage automatic-acceptance ceiling. The broad detector rule is applied to model outputs when computing CS-F1.

\paragraph*{Step 4: Silver-Standard Annotation with an LLM Judge}

Since large-scale manual transcription of dialectal code-switched speech is not practical, AncholVox uses a two-stage silver-standard annotation strategy. We define $\textsc{NSP}(c)$ as the Whisper large-v3 no-speech probability for clip $c$, used as a confidence proxy for speech presence and transcript reliability. Clips with $\textsc{NSP}(c)<\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}=0.30$ are retained automatically as high-confidence speech, following prior evidence that high-confidence Whisper transcripts can provide useful training labels~\cite{yang2025gigaspeech2}. Clips with $\textsc{NSP}(c)\ge\tau_{\mathrm{hi}}=0.60$ are rejected as low-confidence or non-speech clips. The remaining clips, with $0.30 \le \textsc{NSP}(c)<0.60$, are treated as borderline cases.

For the borderline set, Qwen2.5~\cite{qwen2025} evaluates each transcript using
the same local Ollama-based prompt. Open weights make the decision rule
reproducible while keeping transcripts off third-party APIs. The prompt asks
four yes/no questions: whether the transcript is valid Bengali rather than
hallucinated text, whether Bengali--English mixing is natural, whether it
resembles dialectal or informal spoken Bengali, and whether it resembles speech
rather than noise, repeated text, or song lyrics. The implementation returns
\textsc{accept} when three or four criteria pass, \textsc{uncertain} for a
two--two split, and \textsc{reject} when zero or one criterion passes. The
parser derives this outcome from the four criterion values rather than trusting
the model's self-reported overall label. Only
\textsc{accept} clips enter the release.

The stored pre-judge and final manifests make the cascade auditable. Retaining
only $\textsc{NSP}<0.30$ would yield 39{,}772 clips, whereas accepting every
borderline candidate would yield 48{,}484. Qwen2.5 retains 1{,}727 of the
8{,}712 borderline clips (19.8\%) and excludes 6{,}985 (80.2\%), giving the
41{,}499-clip final set. These counts quantify the judge's practical effect, but
not its accuracy. The judge has no access to audio and cannot establish
acoustic correctness; no superiority over a commercial LLM or manual review is
claimed. The two-annotator study in Section~\ref{sec:validation} instead
provides an independent estimate of residual silver-label noise.

\paragraph*{Step 5: Transcript Normalization}

The stored transcripts receive release-time cleaning, including Unicode NFC
normalization, removal of zero-width characters, URLs, hashtags, mentions, and
emojis, digit handling, and punctuation and whitespace cleanup. Scoring is a
separate process. Reported primary WER/CER and all human-reference results use
surface normalization only: they do not apply pronunciation-variant mapping or
repetition collapse. WER-RC/CER-RC add only the stated repetition-collapse
post-processing. An optional normalization routine maps 650 spelling variants
to 271 curated borrowed-word entries, but it is used only for exploratory
analysis and supplies none of the reported primary, WER-RC, or human-reference
numbers.

The pipeline also includes three supporting lexical resources: about 5{,}700
known ASR hallucination and noise tokens for transcript filtering, about
18{,}900 dialectal Bengali word forms protected from standardization, and about
3{,}700 common English words used as evidence in script-aware token labeling.
Their availability does not imply that optional variant mapping was applied to
the reported scores.

\paragraph*{Step 6: Historical Split and Leakage Audit}

All reported predictions and model scores use the archived historical split of 34{,}605 training, 2{,}806 development, and 4{,}088 test clips. A subsequent artifact audit found that this split is not source-disjoint: 69 of the 101 inferred test source IDs also occur elsewhere in the retained corpus, affecting 3{,}772 test clips, and 326 exact-audio duplicate groups cross the historical test boundary. Missing historical train/dev manifests prevent reconstruction of every training assignment, but the observed overlap is sufficient to rule out a leakage-free generalization claim. We therefore retain the archived scores only as diagnostics. After an additional text-quality filter, the frozen repaired partition contains 29{,}224/3{,}855/3{,}297 original train/dev/test clips, with zero inferred-source or exact-audio overlap across partitions. No system in this paper has been retrained on it. Consequently, the paper does not yet provide a leakage-free model comparison; repaired-split retraining and complete metric regeneration remain required future experimental work.

\paragraph*{Step 7: Training Augmentation}

Audio augmentation is applied only to the historical training partition. The augmentation methods include speed perturbation ($\times0.9$ and $\times1.1$), additive noise at 20 dB, and pitch shifting by $\pm2$ semitones, which complement spectrogram-level methods such as SpecAugment~\cite{park2019specaugment}. Augmented clips keep the same parent identifiers and are not deliberately added to development or test. This prevents augmentation-created copies from being assigned across partitions, but it does not repair the pre-existing source and exact-audio overlap in the historical split. After augmentation, the historical training set contains 60{,}936 clips.

Two human annotators listened to the same 100 test clips and transcribed the speech independently without access to each other's annotations. Both annotators completed all 100 clips. The silver transcript is treated as the hypothesis and each human transcript as a reference. Every score in this section, in Table~\ref{tab:validation}, and in Table~\ref{tab:humaneval} is produced by the same \texttt{primary\_normalize} routine that defines the primary condition of the silver-reference benchmark: surface cleaning only, with no pronunciation-variant mapping and no repetition collapse. Human-reference and silver-reference numbers are therefore measured under one identical scoring condition.

Against the first annotator, the silver labels obtain a corpus WER/CER of $0.73/0.44$; against the second annotator, they obtain $0.80/0.49$. These values estimate silver-label noise but should not be interpreted as model accuracy against a fully adjudicated gold reference.

Using the first annotator as the reference, the second annotator has WER/CER of $0.60/0.38$; using the second annotator as the reference, the first annotator has WER/CER of $0.52/0.34$. \subsection{Ethical and Legal Considerations}
The source material is publicly accessible social-media content, but public accessibility alone does not establish permission to redistribute audio or eliminate creators' privacy interests. We therefore retain source identifiers and timestamps, omit speaker names, handles, faces, contact information, and demographic labels, and restrict the intended use to non-commercial ASR research. The corpus contains 1{,}007 source records: 36{,}967 clips in the general collection and 4{,}532 in the UK-diaspora collection. These counts describe collection domains, not population strata.

No consented age, gender, socioeconomic, education, or urban--rural metadata are
available, and we do not infer sensitive attributes from voices or online
profiles. Consequently, AncholVox cannot support demographic subgroup-fairness
claims. Platform visibility, search-query design, and internet access may
overrepresent digitally connected, urban, younger, or diaspora creators; these
characteristics are plausible selection mechanisms, not measured labels.
Dialect coverage therefore reflects online availability rather than population
prevalence. Fig.~\ref{fig:dist} reports the observable dialect imbalance so that
users can apply weighting or resampling, while future consent-based collection
should record self-reported demographic variables for a proper fairness audit.

The release follows a rights-aware governance procedure. It provides a documented takedown and opt-out channel, prohibits speaker identification, surveillance, and re-identification, and records provenance at the source level.

We state the current clearance status explicitly, because describing a governance procedure is not the same as having completed it. Three determinations are treated as blocking for any public redistribution of audio: institutional confirmation of whether formal ethics review is required, legal or institutional review of the redistribution basis for each source platform and content category, and a recorded source-level allow/remove decision. At the time of writing these three determinations are pending, and they are tracked with named owners and dated evidence in the \texttt{ETHICS\_AND\_DATA\_GOVERNANCE.md} register released with the code.

Neither the public accessibility of the source material nor the non-commercial licence we attach to the compilation authorizes redistribution of third-party social-media audio; only those determinations can. Until they are recorded, the audio release is provisional and the intended distribution is the metadata-only fallback: source pointers, timestamps, clip-level split manifests with checksums, transcripts where permitted, and reproducible processing code, without hosted audio. If review later declines a source, that source is removed under a versioned removal log so mirrors can honour the decision.

These safeguards do not remove all risk: speech is potentially identifying, creators did not provide study-specific consent, and models may perform unevenly across dialects. We therefore report these limitations explicitly, provide per-dialect results, and maintain a removal process throughout the lifetime of the resource.

\section{ASR Models and Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:models}
This work evaluates three ASR model families. These models are selected to represent the two main ASR approaches and different types of pre-training.


\paragraph*{Whisper (Sequence-to-Sequence)}

Whisper~\cite{radford2023whisper} is an encoder-decoder transformer model trained on 680k hours of weakly supervised multilingual audio. It is important for this work because its subword vocabulary includes both Bengali and Latin-script tokens. This allows Whisper to generate mixed-script, code-switched output in a single decoding step, making it well suited for Bengali English code-switching. Although convolution-augmented models such as Conformer~\cite{gulati2020conformer} are also strong for ASR, Whisper is especially useful here because of its shared multilingual vocabulary. For fine-tuning, we use Whisper-small, which has 244M parameters. This model is practical to train on modest hardware while still benefiting from large-scale pre-training. During inference, Bengali transcription is forced for all Whisper models.

\paragraph*{Meta MMS (CTC, Multilingual)}

MMS~\cite{pratap2024mms} is a CTC-based multilingual ASR model that supports more than 1{,}000 languages through language-specific adapters. We include it as a strong multilingual zero-shot baseline. However, its Bengali output vocabulary is monolingual, so it cannot generate English-script words. This makes MMS useful for comparison, because it helps show how much code-switching affects models that are not designed to produce mixed-script output.


\paragraph*{wav2vec2-XLS-R (CTC, Self-Supervised)}

wav2vec2-XLS-R-300M~\cite{babu2022xlsr} is a self-supervised multilingual speech model pre-trained on 436k hours of audio. We use a Bengali fine-tuned checkpoint as a zero-shot baseline. We also fine-tune the model on AncholVox so that it can be compared with Whisper using the same training data. This model represents the CTC-based ASR approach in our experiments.

Together, these systems cover multilingual encoder--decoder and CTC paradigms,
monolingual versus mixed-script output vocabularies, and zero-shot versus
in-domain adaptation. They do not constitute an exhaustive leaderboard.
SeamlessM4T is Bengali-capable and would be a useful future comparison, whereas
the released Canary-1B vocabulary does not include Bengali
\cite{seamlessm4t2023,puvvada2024canary}. We therefore restrict conclusions to
the evaluated model set.


\paragraph*{Fine-tuning Setup}
Whisper is fine-tuned using the Hugging Face Transformers~\cite{wolf2020transformers} and Datasets~\cite{lhoest2021datasets} libraries with the Adam optimizer~\cite{kingma2015adam}. We use a learning rate of $10^{-5}$, an effective batch size of 16, three training epochs, and 500 warm-up steps. The best checkpoint is selected based on development WER. For the CTC model, we build a character vocabulary from the training transcripts, covering Bengali, Latin characters, and common punctuation. The CTC output head is reinitialized, and the encoder is fine-tuned for three epochs with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$. All fine-tuning and benchmark inference, including both zero-shot and fine-tuned systems, are run on a single NVIDIA~A100-SXM4-40GB GPU. The one-time silver-standard transcription of all clips using Whisper large-v3 is performed on a CPU workstation with an Intel~Core~i9-14900 processor, 24 cores, and 32~GB RAM.

The CTC model is trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood over all possible alignments $\pi$ that collapse to the target sequence $y$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}} = -\log \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)} \prod_{t} p(\pi_t \mid x)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is the CTC collapsing function. In contrast, Whisper is trained using the standard token-level cross-entropy loss of its decoder. The main hyperparameters are reported in Table~\ref{tab:hparams}. Fig.~\ref{fig:train} shows that all Whisper variants converge within about 2k steps. The CS\_ONLY configuration runs for fewer steps because it uses only the approximately 4.5k code-switched clips.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

This section defines the evaluation metrics, scoring conditions, baseline
systems, and statistical tests used for dialectal and code-switched Bengali
speech.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We use Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) as the main evaluation metrics. Because the benchmark references are automatic silver labels, these scores measure surface agreement with the silver reference, not definitive transcription accuracy. CER is especially important in this work because dialectal Bengali does not have a standardized spelling system. As a result, WER may penalize valid transcriptions that use different spellings for the same spoken word, while CER measures character-level agreement with the same noisy reference.

To characterize code-switched speech, we report WER separately for Bengali-script and English-script reference words within the 176 silver-labelled code-switched clips (WER-BN-CS and WER-EN-CS). These are not scores for Bengali-only or English-only clips. Both are computed on the same code-switched subset, so they share a scope with the language-balanced WER (LB-WER) defined as the unweighted mean
\begin{equation}
\text{LB-WER}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{WER-BN-CS}+\text{WER-EN-CS}).
\end{equation}
Keeping all three quantities on one subset is what makes the identity above hold in the reported tables; computing the components corpus-wide and the mean on the CS subset would combine incompatible scopes. LB-WER gives the two scripts equal influence despite unequal token counts; it does not test a joint word-and-language label.

Code-Switch Detection F1 (CS-F1) measures whether the hypothesis contains both Bengali and English according to the script-based detector. Unlike the metrics above, CS-F1 is a \emph{clip-level binary classification} score evaluated over the entire test set: all 4{,}088 clips are classified, of which 176 are code-switched positives and 3{,}912 are negatives. Precision therefore depends on false positives raised on Bengali-only clips, which is the intended behaviour --- a system that emits spurious Latin tokens everywhere should be penalized. CS-F1 is thus not restricted to the 176-clip subset, even though its recall term and the confidence intervals we report are governed by that small positive count.

We distinguish two scoring conditions. The \emph{primary} condition applies conventional surface normalization (Unicode normalization, case handling, punctuation removal, and whitespace cleanup) while retaining every repeated character and word, so decoder insertion errors remain in WER and CER. The \emph{WER-RC/CER-RC} condition additionally collapses excessive character and consecutive-word repetitions and is reported as explicit system post-processing. All models use the same procedure within each condition.

Formally, WER is calculated using substitutions, deletions, and insertions:

\begin{equation}
\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}
\end{equation}

where $S$, $D$, and $I$ represent substitution, deletion, and insertion errors, and $N$ is the total number of words.
WER can exceed $1.0$ when insertion errors are large; in this work, that happens on a small number of hallucination-heavy zero-shot outputs, so values above $1.0$ are expected and do not indicate a scoring bug.

CER is calculated in the same way but at the character level:

\begin{equation}
\text{CER} = \frac{S_c + D_c + I_c}{N_c}
\end{equation}

CER complements WER because dialectal spellings may differ even when their character sequences remain similar; it is not treated as a replacement for human-reference evaluation.

For code-switch detection, we treat it as a yes/no problem: whether code-switching appears in the output or not. We use Precision, Recall, and F1 score:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{P} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \quad
\mathrm{R} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \quad
\text{CS-F1} = \frac{2PR}{P + R}
\end{equation}

Finally, English-word survival (EN-Surv) measures how many English words in the reference are correctly preserved in the model output for code-switched speech:

\begin{equation}
\text{EN-Surv} =
\frac{\sum_{(r,h)\in\mathcal{P}} |\{w \in r : \mathrm{English}(w)\} \cap h|}
{\sum_{(r,h)\in\mathcal{P}} |\{w \in r : \mathrm{English}(w)\}|}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of code-switched (reference, hypothesis) pairs.

In the discussion below, EN-Surv is our primary code-switching metric because it directly measures English-token retention. WER-CS, WER-BN-CS, WER-EN-CS and LB-WER are reported as supporting diagnostics computed on the smaller 176-clip code-switched test subset; CS-F1 is a detection score over the whole 4{,}088-clip test set, and only its 176 positives limit the precision of its recall term.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare our method with four zero-shot baseline models. These include
Whisper-small (used as a base model), Whisper large-v3 (the largest evaluated
Whisper checkpoint), MMS-1B, and wav2vec2-XLS-R-BN, which is trained on standard
Bengali speech.

Whisper large-v3 also generated the silver labels, so its 0.930 evaluation WER
requires an artifact-level explanation. The two relevant comparisons are
distinct. First, treating each archived construction-time transcript as the
reference and the paired evaluation-time large-v3 output as the hypothesis
gives 0.927 WER. Second, comparing the same evaluation-time outputs with the
stored silver references gives 0.930 WER. By contrast, construction-time
transcripts and stored silver references differ by only 0.005 WER after final
text cleaning.

The current source paths are materially different: construction defaults to
faster-whisper/CTranslate2 large-v3 with beam size 1,
\texttt{condition\_on\_previous\_text=False}, word timestamps, and no VAD;
benchmark inference uses Hugging Face Transformers, batches of 16, forced
Bengali transcription, and a 128-new-token cap. Current scripts record package
versions, requested and resolved revisions where available, decoding metadata,
and audio SHA-256 values on new runs. The archived rows contain none of those
fields, however, so neither historical model revision nor historical software
versions can be recovered. Present local WAV hashes likewise cannot prove the
bytes used in remote historical inference. Artifact hashes and the precise
known/unknown provenance fields are released in
\texttt{REFERENCE\_PIPELINE\_PROVENANCE.md}. The discrepancy therefore remains
unresolved and is not treated as a self-reproduction check.

\subsection{Significance Testing}

To quantify uncertainty, we use two-sided paired bootstrap tests~\cite{berg2012empirical} with 10{,}000 clip-level resamples. For each resample, corpus WER is recomputed from the summed edit and reference-word counts. The two-sided $p$-value is twice the smaller bootstrap tail probability around zero, with a plus-one Monte Carlo correction; percentile intervals provide 95\% confidence intervals. Tests use a fixed seed and are reported separately for primary WER and WER-RC. Clips cut from the same or related source videos are not statistically independent, so clip resampling can understate uncertainty. The released analysis also reports exploratory source-video-cluster resampling, but inferred source IDs do not establish speaker independence and the contaminated historical split remains unresolved. We consequently treat all significance tests involving fine-tuned systems as diagnostic rather than as generalization evidence.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}
This section analyzes model behavior beyond overall error rates. It examines error patterns, code-switching behavior, and qualitative failure cases across different models and dialects. It also evaluates how well models preserve code-switching structure, compares model outputs with human references, and reports statistical significance. The goal is to characterize the observed differences and identify which parts of dialectal and code-switched ASR remain difficult.

\subsection{Code-Switching Error Analysis}

We first clarify the scope of the code-switching results. The per-clip CS error rates --- WER-CS, WER-BN-CS, WER-EN-CS and LB-WER --- are computed on only 176 code-switched test clips. WER-BN-CS means Bengali-script words within those mixed clips; it is distinct from whole-clip WER on the 3{,}912 Bengali-only clips, denoted WER-BN-only below. CS-F1 is scoped differently: every one of the 4{,}088 test clips is classified as code-switched or not, so its false positives come from the Bengali-only clips even though only 176 positives are available to estimate recall. Therefore, these results should be treated as indicative rather than highly precise. The strongest descriptive pattern is that monolingual and Bengali-only models almost completely drop exact English tokens, but this too requires confirmation against adjudicated references.

Because only 176 test clips are labeled as code-switched, whole-clip WER-CS and
the recall side of CS-F1 are treated as descriptive supporting measures rather
than definitive rankings. English-word survival provides a directly interpretable complementary
measure, but it likewise requires confirmation on a larger adjudicated subset.

Bootstrap intervals from the existing predictions quantify this uncertainty.
For Whisper ALL, WER-CS is 0.959 (95\% CI $[0.868,1.063]$), CS-F1 is 0.192
($[0.120,0.264]$), and English-word survival is 0.127
($[0.064,0.201]$). CS\_ONLY has the higher CS-F1, 0.276
($[0.220,0.328]$). The paired CS-F1 difference for ALL minus BN\_ONLY is
$+0.181$ ($[0.111,0.253]$), whereas ALL minus CS\_ONLY is $-0.083$
($[-0.153,-0.015]$). Thus, on these historical predictions, ALL has higher
CS-F1 than BN\_ONLY but lower CS-F1 than CS\_ONLY; this is a descriptive recipe
comparison rather than a causal training effect.

Table~\ref{tab:csgap} gives a descriptive comparison of primary WER on
code-switched and Bengali-only clips, and Fig.~\ref{fig:cs} shows the same
contrast alongside the detection scores. Most gaps are small or negative, so this
comparison does not support a causal claim that the CS subset is intrinsically
harder; the subsets differ in content and length. The clearer finding is
token-specific: English survival is near zero for monolingual and BN\_ONLY
systems, while CS-supervised Whisper systems recover more mixed-script output.

Beyond checking whether English words are preserved, we compare the number of language transitions produced by each system. Walking through a transcript from left to right, we label every language-bearing token as Bengali or English and count a \emph{switch} at each adjacent pair whose labels differ; the 176 code-switched test clips contain 463 reference switches. The count-recall diagnostic is $\sum_c \min(\hat{s}_c, s_c)/\sum_c s_c$, where $s_c$ and $\hat{s}_c$ are the reference and hypothesis switch counts of clip $c$; the cap limits rewards for over-generation. This statistic compares counts only: it does not determine whether a switch occurred at the correct word or boundary, so it is not a positional-preservation score. We report it only for the Whisper sequence-to-sequence models, because they can naturally generate mixed Bengali and Latin script. Monolingual CTC models such as MMS are excluded because they cannot reliably produce Latin-script words, with EN-survival of $0.00$ in Table~\ref{tab:errors}. To reduce character-level artifacts, a Latin token is counted as English only if it contains at least two letters.

Table~\ref{tab:switch} reports this count-based analysis. Reference code-switched clips
contain an average of 2.63 language switches, and every Whisper system produces
fewer. Within the fine-tuned family, preservation
tracks the amount of code-switch supervision exactly as expected: CS\_ONLY
preserves 36\% of reference switches, ALL 12\%, and BN\_ONLY almost none (2\%).
Zero-shot large-v3 is highest overall (46\%), consistent with it also having the
highest English survival in Table~\ref{tab:errors}. A model can match the count
while placing switches at the wrong words, and the measure may still be
inflated by over-generation that the cap does not fully remove. It is therefore
reported as a descriptive count-recall upper bound, not evidence that the
correct switching positions were preserved. Relative to BN\_ONLY, the
joint-data recipe is associated with higher code-switch detection
(Table~\ref{tab:main}); relative to CS\_ONLY, however, it has lower switch-count
recall.

\subsection{Qualitative Error Analysis}

Inspection of model outputs shows three repeated error patterns. For readability, the examples below use romanized Bengali and omit Bengali script.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Repetition hallucination occurs when Whisper generates repeated words on
difficult clips. For example, a short reference such as ``\textit{tai}'' may be
expanded into many repeated tokens. Primary WER counts every insertion; WER-RC
reports the behavior of an explicitly post-processed system.

\item English deletion occurs when monolingual or Bengali-focused models remove English words from code-switched speech. For example, the reference ``\textit{ami office-e meeting korlam}'' may be decoded as ``\textit{ami $\dots$ korlam}'', where the English words are missing. This explains the near-zero English-word survival shown in Table~\ref{tab:errors}.

\item Spelling variation occurs when fine-tuned models produce phonetically correct words using different spellings, such as ``\textit{kortasi}'' and ``\textit{kortechi}'' for the same spoken form. These cases are counted as word-level errors, but they do not always indicate real recognition failure. This also explains why CER is much lower than WER.
\end{itemize}

These patterns explain the main trends in the results. Fine-tuning mainly reduces hallucination and English-word deletion, while many remaining errors come from acceptable spelling differences caused by the lack of standardized dialect spelling.

Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} expresses substitutions, deletions, and insertions as
shares of all primary word errors. Large-v3 has a 48\% deletion share. For ALL,
the deletion share falls to 12\%, but the insertion share rises to 32\%, exposing
the repetition behavior hidden by WER-RC. Approximately 14--15\% of errors in
the fine-tuned systems satisfy the near-spelling threshold, against 5\% for
zero-shot large-v3, so fine-tuning shifts error mass from outright deletion
toward orthographic near-misses. These are reported as potential orthographic
variants, not automatically relabeled as correct.

\subsection{Case Study}

A single Sylheti test clip illustrates decoder repetition: a one-word reference
is expanded by zero-shot Whisper-small into roughly forty repeated tokens.
Primary scoring correctly counts these as insertions. Repetition-collapse
post-processing shortens such degenerate runs, explaining the WER-RC change in
Table~\ref{tab:ablnorm}; it does not constitute an improvement to the underlying
acoustic model. A second recurring failure is English-token deletion by
monolingual and Bengali-only systems.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}

Because the historical split is contaminated and clips from related source
videos are dependent, the following clip-bootstrap results are secondary
descriptive evidence, not confirmatory tests of generalization. Exploratory
source-video-cluster resampling is included in the released report, but it
cannot undo train--test source overlap or establish speaker independence.

Under that limited interpretation, the primary-WER bootstrap reports lower ALL
error than Whisper-small ($p=0.0002$) and MMS-1B ($p=0.0066$), no detectable
difference from the zero-shot wav2vec2 Bengali checkpoint ($p=0.9411$), and
higher error than large-v3 ($p=0.0012$), fine-tuned wav2vec2 ($p=0.0002$), and
BN\_ONLY ($p=0.0002$). For WER-RC, ALL has a 95\% interval of
$[0.789,0.813]$; its clip-bootstrap difference from BN\_ONLY is not detectable
($p=0.9109$). Code-switch intervals are wider because the test set contains
only 176 transcript-labeled positive clips. The estimates therefore describe
the archived artifacts and do not establish a universal model ranking.


\subsection{Interpreting High Error Rates}

Primary WER remains high for every system, ranging from 0.861 to 1.142. Values
above 1.0 arise when insertions exceed the number of reference words. This
reflects difficult dialectal speech, non-standard spelling, decoder failures,
and noise in Whisper-derived silver references. CER and WER-RC illuminate
specific aspects of that difficulty, but neither removes the need for primary
WER and human-reference evaluation. AncholVox is therefore positioned as a
stress-test benchmark with transparent scoring conditions.


\subsection{Human-Reference Evaluation}
\label{sec:humaneval}

Since the test labels are silver-standard, the main benchmark may favor systems
whose output resembles Whisper. We therefore evaluate every model against both
human transcripts in the 100-clip validation sample, under the same primary
scoring condition used for Table~\ref{tab:main}. The ranking changes
substantially. MMS-1B has the lowest WER and CER against both annotators
($0.76/0.39$ and $0.70/0.36$), followed by fine-tuned wav2vec2; the whole
Whisper family, including the proposed joint-data system, falls behind both.
Against the first annotator the three Whisper training configurations are effectively tied
($0.92$--$0.93$), so the CS/BN/ALL ordering seen on the silver benchmark does
not survive a human reference. This ranking change is important evidence of
reference-dependent bias and is reported as a central result rather than a
secondary caveat.

The fine-tuned Whisper systems have lower error than zero-shot Whisper-small
under both human references, and MMS-1B's result motivates a
larger adjudicated evaluation across model families. Because the current sample
contains only 100 clips and two independent, non-adjudicated transcripts, the
human ranking is informative but not definitive; this is a limited validation
study rather than a conclusive model-ranking experiment.

\subsection{LLM-Ensemble Diagnostic}

After the human-reference evaluation, we include a secondary text-only
diagnostic using Mistral-7B~\cite{jiang2023mistral}, LLaMA-3.1-8B~\cite{dubey2024llama},
and Gemma-3-12B~\cite{gemmateam2025gemma3technicalreport}. The judges score the
same fixed, released 50-clip list (25 transcript-labeled CS and 25 Bengali-only
clips) for semantic plausibility, apparent code-switch preservation, and
dialect naturalness. They see neither audio nor a reference transcript.

BN\_ONLY has the highest nominal overall score (2.89/5), followed by ALL
(2.81), CS\_ONLY (2.68), and large-v3 (2.47); the ALL--BN\_ONLY paired
difference is not detectable on this sample ($p=0.1532$). More importantly,
BN\_ONLY receives the highest apparent code-switch score despite an EN-Surv of

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the claims of this study. First, the transcripts are
\emph{silver-standard}: they are generated by Whisper large-v3 and textually
filtered by Qwen2.5, which cannot inspect the audio. The two-annotator
validation on 100 clips estimates silver-label WER/CER at $0.73/0.44$ and
$0.80/0.49$ against the two independent references, but it is neither large nor
adjudicated. Because Whisper generated the silver references, rankings may also
favor Whisper-like outputs. The Qwen cascade is reproducible and its retention
rate is reported, but its accept/reject accuracy has not been independently
human-labeled. The archived construction snapshot stores overall Qwen votes but
not all four criterion values; the released script now records those values and
derives the decision deterministically. A regenerated release should therefore
be versioned rather than assumed to be byte-identical to the archived snapshot.

Second, the script-based detector labels 2{,}361 of 41{,}499 silver transcripts
(5.7\%) as code-switched, with only 176 positives in the test set. The reported bootstrap intervals
expose the resulting uncertainty, and the CS findings should not be generalized
beyond Bengali--English switching in these social-media domains. Third, the
baseline suite is representative rather than exhaustive. Larger fine-tuned
models and Bengali-capable SeamlessM4T may change absolute rankings; Canary-1B
is not a Bengali baseline. No cross-corpus evaluation on Ben-10, Common Voice,
or another external Bengali test set was run, so this study makes no claim of
out-of-domain generalization.

The archived historical split is also not source-disjoint. Sixty-nine of 101
inferred test sources occur elsewhere in the retained corpus, and 326 exact-
audio duplicate groups cross the test boundary. Consequently, fine-tuned model
scores cannot be interpreted as leakage-free generalization. A repaired split
is frozen, but the systems require retraining before it can support new model
comparisons. Within the historical test set, clips from the same source video
are dependent; clip-level bootstrap intervals may therefore be too narrow.
Released source-cluster analyses address that unit of dependence only
partially, because source IDs neither prove speaker independence nor repair
cross-split contamination.

The large-v3 reference/evaluation discrepancy is likewise not fully
reconstructible. With construction-time transcripts as references, the paired
evaluation-time outputs differ at 0.927 WER; against the stored silver
references, those same outputs obtain 0.930 WER. The current code shows
different inference backends and decoding settings, but missing historical
revisions, software versions, and paired audio hashes prevent a unique causal
diagnosis. Current-environment details and available artifact hashes are
archived separately in 
\texttt{REFERENCE\_PIPELINE\_PROVENANCE.md}; they are not attributed to the
historical runs.

Finally, dialect and source domain are the only available audit dimensions.
The absence of consented demographic metadata prevents age-, gender-,
socioeconomic-, and urban--rural fairness analyses. Search and platform
selection may overrepresent more visible, digitally connected speakers, and the
dialect distribution is imbalanced. The informal vlog, comedy, review, and
diaspora domains also differ from broadcast news, healthcare, and education.
The LLM-ensemble semantic scores add a meaning-preservation diagnostic, but
they remain model-based and cannot substitute for human judgments; being
reference-free and text-only, they also cannot detect content that the model
silently omitted, which is why code-switching fidelity is claimed only from the
reference-based metrics. These
limitations motivate consent-based demographic auditing, targeted CS
collection, external-corpus testing, and a larger adjudicated human set when resources
permit.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

AncholVox contributes a silver-standard diagnostic benchmark rather than a new
state-of-the-art ASR model. All model comparisons use a contaminated historical
split and therefore do not constitute leakage-free generalization results. On
that split, fine-tuned wav2vec2 has the lowest primary silver-reference WER
(0.861), whereas the limited 100-clip human-reference study gives MMS-1B the
lowest WER against both annotators. The changed ordering demonstrates that the
rankings are reference-dependent.

The Whisper recipes describe trade-offs rather than a single winner. Compared
with BN\_ONLY, ALL has higher transcript-derived CS-F1 (0.192 versus 0.011) and
English-token survival (0.13 versus 0.01), but CS\_ONLY is higher on both
switching measures and has worse overall silver-reference WER. Because these
recipes differ in coverage, sample count, and optimization budget, the
differences are descriptive associations, not causal estimates of training-data
composition. Repetition collapse likewise changes the deployed output condition
rather than the acoustic model and is therefore reported separately.

The benchmark is useful for studying silver-reference bias, regional variation,
English-token retention, and decoder repetition under transparent validity
limits. Priorities for future work are retraining on the repaired source-disjoint
split, expanding and adjudicating the human test set, completing rights review,
testing external Bengali corpora and Bengali-capable architectures, improving
repetition-aware decoding, and conducting consent-based demographic auditing.


\section*{Data and Code Availability}

The dataset landing page and code are available at
\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/niloycste68/Bangali_local_dialect_ASR_HF_Dataset}
and \url{https://github.com/niloycste/ASR-Bengali-Local-Dialect-}. The public
resource name is AncholVox; these legacy URL slugs are retained to avoid
breaking existing links. Audio is
under gated access: the release register (\texttt{ETHICS\_AND\_DATA\_GOVERNANCE.md}
in the code repository) records three blocking determinations --- institutional
ethics confirmation, redistribution review, and per-source allow/remove
decisions --- none of which has yet been made, and hosted audio is not publicly
downloadable until they are. Describing this procedure is not a claim to have
completed it; the register states its status as of the submission date. The repository
documents the multi-stage commands, augmentation step, model identifiers,
hyperparameters, split settings, fixed statistical seed, dependency lock file,
and checkpoint manifest. Released prediction files reproduce the reported
metrics, confidence intervals, and two-sided $p$-values without retraining.

The historical test manifest on which every reported number depends is archived
in full: 4{,}088 clips, 176 transcript-labelled code-switched clips, and 13.2
hours, matching Table~\ref{tab:splits}. Historical train and development
manifests are absent, and the audit shows that the historical test set is not
source-disjoint from the retained remainder. We therefore do not describe the
archived scores as leakage-free. For future retraining, the construction code
now sorts inferred sources before seeded assignment, and a separate repaired
split is frozen as original-clip manifests with SHA-256 checksums under
\texttt{dataset\_pipeline/repaired\_split\_manifests}. The historical and
repaired partitions must not be conflated; no result from the repaired split is
reported in this paper.
End-to-end checkpoint reuse additionally requires depositing the large trained
weights at the archived locations listed in the manifest. Audio redistribution
is governed by the rights-review procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset};
where redistribution is not authorized, the release is limited to permissible
metadata, source pointers, and processing code.
